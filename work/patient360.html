Patient 360: one system, two clinicians.

A self-initiated concept for a medical-records platform, taken end to end. The core idea: one shared patient record that reshapes itself for whoever logs in — a nurse and a doctor see the right information and tools for their part of the same consultation, over a single source of truth.

The context

Help practitioners make better decisions, faster.

I imagined a fast-growing medical-software company that wants the best all-in-one platform for practitioners — bookings, registration, records, clinical notes, claims and billing. The problem I chose to explore: help clinicians make better decisions by accessing and capturing vital clinical information — history, examination notes, procedures, prescriptions and lab reports — on a returning patient.

Find fast

Locate a specific patient across ~20 identifiers — name, patient number, DOB, contact and more.

See the whole picture

An overview of the patient's medical history and all clinical information in one place.

Capture in context

Take notes during a consultation without losing the thread of the conversation.

Desktop first

A connected desktop surface as the primary workspace for the core clinical use cases.

A deliberate scope. Of three possible scenarios — consulting, admitted-patient, and emergency — I focused on the doctor–patient consultation with a returning patient, because it encompasses the core use cases without boiling the ocean.

The constraint

A compliance-heavy, privacy-first domain — explored honestly.

Healthcare is high-stakes: data protection, consent, and clinical compliance are design constraints, not footnotes. As a self-directed concept I couldn't run primary research, so I grounded it in secondary research and conversations with contacts in healthcare — captured as explicit assumptions rather than hidden guesses.

Privacy by design

Informed consent, data minimisation, the right to access, and breach-aware handling shape what's shown and to whom.

Navigating chaos

Clinicians multitask and context-switch constantly; the interface has to stay calm and show the right thing at the right moment.

Pen to pixel

Users range from tech-savvy to traditional — many still prefer pen and paper — so the flow must feel natural to both.

Lens 01 · Rigor

Two roles, one journey, every need traced.

I framed the two clinicians by their jobs to be done rather than rigid personas, since their roles overlap around the same patient. That produced a shared consultation journey and a needs matrix that keeps every UI decision traceable to a real need.

Doctor · JTBD

Understand → Diagnose → Treat

Understand the issue and history, capture findings, synthesise a diagnosis, and put a treatment plan into action.

Pain: information overload · fragmented history · the burden of documentation.

Nurse · JTBD

Prepare → Vitals → Coordinate → Facilitate

Verify the patient, capture vitals, execute the doctor's orders, and help the patient understand next steps.

Pain: task juggling · constant interruptions · communication delays.

The framing

How might we help clinicians decide with the right information?

01

A scannable story

Turn a fragmented medical history into a clear story a doctor can grasp at a glance.

02

Documenting = talking

Make capturing a consultation feel as natural as the conversation itself, in context.

03

Fast, error-proof

Help a nurse capture vitals and update core info in the fastest, most error-proof way.

Lens 02 · Vision

One record that reshapes for whoever logs in.

Rather than build two products, the vision is a single system with a shared patient core and role-adaptive layouts. The nurse view leads with what a nurse does — notes for the doctor and a fast vitals capture. The doctor view reshapes the very same record to lead with an AI summary, the nurse's notes, and a consultation flow.

The same patient, two layouts. Nothing is duplicated — components are re-ordered and re-prioritised over one shared source of truth, so the vitals a nurse captures surface automatically in the doctor's view.

Lens 03 · Craft

The consultation, end to end.

The loop closes

The trade-off

One adaptive system vs. two tailored tools.

The tension

Two roles, two very different workflows. The easy path is two separate tools — each perfectly tailored, but with duplicated data, drifting sources of truth, and a broken hand-off between nurse and doctor.

The bet

One shared core that flexes — persona layouts, re-ordered components, and focus modes. It costs more design thought up front, but keeps data consistent and makes the nurse→doctor→nurse hand-off feel like one continuous system.

Outcome

What the exploration proved.

Reduce cognitive load

Clinicians multitask — so the interface stays clear and surfaces relevant info in context, role by role.

Role-based, one core

Roles differ but the core data is the same; persona layouts + re-ordering tailor the experience without forking the product.

Reduce data entry

Connected data, smart autocomplete and structured records cut the repetitive typing that clinicians resent.

Closing

What I'd carry forward.

Design the hand-off

The magic isn't either screen — it's that a nurse's work reappears exactly where the doctor needs it, and back again.

Adapt, don't duplicate

One shared core with role-adaptive layouts beats two tailored tools that drift apart.

Trace every pixel

A needs → data → function matrix kept the UI honest — every component earns its place from a real need.
